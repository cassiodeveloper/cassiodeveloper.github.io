AppSec, Motherfucker: a manifesto against security theater

Why AppSec must move beyond compliance theater and return to honest conversations about decisions, engineering and real risk.

AppSec needs to start telling the truth again

Many organizations say they practice Application Security because they run a scanner, receive a penetration test report or have an approval step in the pipeline.

That does not mean the software is safer.

AppSec, Motherfucker is a manifesto against security treated as process decoration. It exists to provoke those who have turned secure development into a checklist, dashboard or compliance ritual with no practical consequence.

The problem is not a lack of tools

Tools are necessary. SAST, DAST, SCA and other technologies help find problems at a scale manual analysis could never reach.

The mistake begins when the tool's output becomes the objective.

A scanner can produce thousands of findings while the organization still does not know:

  • which applications carry the most risk;
  • who should fix each problem;
  • which findings matter in the current context;
  • what should actually block a delivery;
  • whether a corrected vulnerability is gone from production.

A tool is a sensor. AppSec is the system of decisions, responsibilities and actions built around that sensor.

Compliance is not the destination

Compliance can establish a baseline, require evidence and make responsibilities visible. Passing an audit, however, does not guarantee that the right technical decisions are being made.

Real security appears in design, code, pipelines, operations and incident response. It must work under deadline pressure, around legacy systems and when a finding does not have an obvious answer.

The manifesto questions the distance between the documented process and the process that actually runs.

Who the manifesto is for

This is for:

  • AppSec professionals tired of producing reports nobody uses;
  • developers who receive findings without context;
  • managers who confuse alert volume with risk reduction;
  • teams that added scanners to a pipeline and called it DevSecOps;
  • organizations that want a more honest security conversation.

It is not a maturity framework or a ready-made implementation methodology. It is an invitation to stop operating on autopilot and examine what current practices actually deliver.

How to use the provocation

Read it, disagree with it and bring the questions to your team.

Observe the pipeline that runs, not only the diagram. Choose one important finding and follow its complete path to remediation. Verify who decides, who receives context, how long it takes and how the solution is validated.

If the process cannot answer those questions, another tool will probably not solve the problem.

The name is intentionally uncomfortable. Security theater should be uncomfortable too.
